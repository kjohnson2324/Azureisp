THE UNWITTING INSIDER


The traditional definition of insider threat assumes a human actor: a disgruntled employee, a negligent contractor, a compromised privileged user. That definition has been under pressure for months as AI agents have accumulated legitimate access to enterprise data. This week, a research disclosure from Varonis made the pressure concrete.

The vulnerability, named SearchLeak and tracked as CVE-2026-42824, allowed an attacker to turn Microsoft 365 Copilot into a data exfiltration tool with a single click. [1] Emails, OneDrive files, SharePoint documents, meeting notes, password reset links, and security codes were all reachable through Copilot's legitimate search access, redirected to an attacker-controlled destination through a three-stage exploit chain. Microsoft patched the vulnerability in June 2026. But the attack technique it demonstrated is not a one-time event. It is a template for a class of attack that will recur as long as AI systems hold broad legitimate access to enterprise data.

Microsoft's response on the defensive side came through Purview. Insider Risk Management for risky agents, which entered public preview in December 2025, is now on track for general availability in July 2026, with policies specifically designed to detect and govern exactly the class of risk that SearchLeak represents. [2] The timing is not a coincidence. The threat and the platform response are arriving together.


WHAT SEARCHLEAK SHOWED US


The SearchLeak exploit chain combined three distinct vulnerabilities, each unremarkable on its own. A parameter-to-prompt injection allowed an attacker to embed malicious instructions in content that Copilot would process. An HTML rendering race condition let those instructions execute before the content security policy could evaluate and block them. A Bing server-side request forgery bypass provided the exfiltration channel: data retrieved through Copilot's legitimate search access was forwarded externally before the CSP enforcement caught up. [1]

The end result was that a legitimate Microsoft link, when clicked by a target, would silently invoke Copilot's search capabilities against the target's mailbox, files, and calendar, and transmit the results outward. The user saw nothing. The Copilot session appeared normal. The access was fully authorized because Copilot legitimately held that access.

This is what makes SearchLeak analytically important beyond the specific CVE. The attack did not require stealing credentials, elevating privileges, or bypassing authentication. It leveraged access that had already been granted. The threat actor used the AI system as a proxy for the legitimate access the user had been given, with no action required from the user beyond clicking a link.

Varonis describes this as turning Copilot into an "unwitting insider." That framing is precise. The system did what it was designed to do: retrieve information the user could access and fulfill a query. The query happened to originate from an attacker rather than the user. At the time of execution, Copilot could not distinguish between the two. [1]

Three security architecture implications follow from this.

First, data overpermissioning in Microsoft 365 is now a direct attack surface amplification factor. If Copilot can access everything a user can access, and a user has access to more than they need, then SearchLeak-class attacks expand their blast radius to match the overpermission. Organizations that have not completed least-privilege access reviews on SharePoint sites, OneDrive folders, and mailbox permissions are carrying more exposure than they may realize.

Second, prompt injection is the mechanism this class of attack requires. The malicious instructions embedded in external content are what direct Copilot's legitimate capabilities toward illegitimate ends. This is the same technique behind a growing family of attacks across AI-integrated products, and it is not fully solved by patching individual CVEs. Each new Copilot integration creates a new prompt injection surface.

Third, because the access involved is legitimate, traditional DLP controls that focus on unauthorized data movement will miss this class of attack entirely. The data movement is authorized from Copilot's perspective. Detection requires behavioral analysis of what Copilot was asked to retrieve, not just what it moved.


WHAT THE PLATFORM PROVIDES


Three capabilities map directly onto the risk picture SearchLeak has drawn.

1. CVE-2026-42824: verify the patch is applied and assess the access footprint. Microsoft patched SearchLeak server-side in June 2026, meaning no client-side action is required from end users. [1] But the remediation action for security teams is not limited to confirming the patch. SearchLeak is a prompt to answer a question most organizations have not answered: what data does Copilot have access to on behalf of each user, and how much of that access is actually necessary? The practical steps are a SharePoint and OneDrive permission review scoped to content indexed by Copilot, and an evaluation of which organizational data sources have been connected to Copilot through Graph or plugin integrations. That review was warranted before SearchLeak. SearchLeak makes it urgent.

2. Purview Insider Risk Management for risky agents, reaching general availability in July 2026. Microsoft Purview IRM has extended its policy framework to cover AI agents and Copilot activity, with risky AI usage policies, agent workflow governance, and integration with Copilot Studio and Azure AI Foundry. [2] The capability detects anomalous Copilot query patterns, unusual data volume retrievals through agent interactions, and sequences of activity that match known insider risk indicators. For organizations already running IRM policies for human insider risk, the agent extension applies the same policy logic to AI-driven activity. The GA timeline of July 2026 means security teams should be preparing the policy configuration now rather than waiting for the feature to flip on. The Purview IRM documentation for risky agents includes a set of starter policies that can be adapted to most organizational contexts. What the starter policies do not include is organizational context: which Copilot interactions represent normal usage in your environment, and which represent anomalous volume or data access. That baseline requires time to build, and it should be started before GA arrives.

3. Prompt injection detection across Defender and Purview. The underlying technique in SearchLeak, embedding malicious instructions in content that an AI system will process, is addressable through a combination of controls already available in the Microsoft platform. Purview Communication Compliance can be configured to flag external content that contains known prompt injection patterns before it reaches a Copilot-accessible mailbox or SharePoint location. Defender for Office 365 Safe Links provides some protection against the malicious link that initiates the SearchLeak chain. Defender for Cloud Apps behavioral analytics surfaces anomalous Copilot data retrieval volumes that may indicate an active exploit. None of these controls stops prompt injection categorically. Each reduces the probability of successful exploitation and shortens the detection window when exploitation occurs.


REDEFINING THE INSIDER


The Varonis research team chose the word "insider" deliberately. They are describing a structural shift in how insider risk needs to be understood: the insider is no longer only a human. Any system with legitimate access to organizational data that can be directed by an external party is an insider risk surface.

Microsoft's framing in the Purview IRM extension reaches the same conclusion from the policy direction. The IRM policies for risky agents do not treat AI systems as external threats. They treat them as principals capable of insider-risk-class behavior, with the same need for behavioral monitoring, anomaly detection, and policy governance that applies to human users with elevated access.

This is the right frame. It is also a materially more complex governance problem than traditional IRM. Human insiders have a defined identity, a role, and a history of activity. AI agents can be invoked by any user with access, can act on instructions embedded in external content, and leave an audit trail that is harder to interpret than human activity logs.

The combination of SearchLeak class vulnerability disclosures and the Purview IRM extension for agents is the beginning of the industry working through what governance of the AI insider surface actually requires. The organizations that engage with that question deliberately will have a more defensible posture than the ones that treat it as a future problem.


THE ROAD AHEAD


SearchLeak is patched. The technique it demonstrated is not. As Microsoft expands Copilot's integration surface across Teams, Outlook, SharePoint, and the growing ecosystem of agent-connected data sources, each integration creates a new surface for prompt injection and a new potential exfiltration vector for the class of attack SearchLeak proved out.

The defensive posture that will matter over the next 12 months is not primarily about patching individual CVEs in Copilot, though that matters. It is about access hygiene at the data layer, prompt injection detection at the content layer, and behavioral monitoring at the agent activity layer. Purview IRM for risky agents addresses the third. The first two require decisions about permissions and content governance that sit outside the IRM product entirely.

The organizations that will manage this risk effectively are the ones that treat Copilot access scope as a security control, not just a productivity configuration.


FOR DISCUSSION

1. Has your organization completed a review of what data Copilot can reach on behalf of your users? Does that access reflect least-privilege principles, or does it inherit the full overpermissioned breadth of typical SharePoint and OneDrive configurations?

2. Prompt injection is the underlying technique in SearchLeak and a growing family of AI-targeted attacks. Does your current security architecture include any controls specifically designed to detect or prevent prompt injection attempts in content that Copilot or your organization's agents will process?

3. Purview Insider Risk Management for risky agents reaches general availability in July 2026. Is your IRM program prepared to extend its policy framework to cover AI agent and Copilot activity, and does your team have a plan for building the behavioral baseline before GA arrives?
