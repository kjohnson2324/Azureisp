WHAT SURVIVES THE RESET


Researchers at Hunt.io reconstructed a campaign called Operation CameraSwarm from a 407 megabyte working directory an attacker had left exposed, containing tooling, logs, shell history, and campaign records covering more than 14,530 compromised Dahua IoT cameras across Ukraine and Russia. [1] The detail worth dwelling on is not the scale. It is that on 1,923 of those cameras, the attacker planted a backdoor account stored independently of the administrator password, one that survives both a password reset and, on most firmware, a full factory reset. [2] The standard incident response instinct, wipe it and start clean, does not work against this specific technique.

Microsoft's own August 27 roundup of new security capabilities describes a different kind of persistence problem, this one organizational rather than technical. Microsoft Entra Tenant Governance, which helps organizations discover unmanaged shadow tenants and apply consistent policy across them, reached general availability. [3] Alongside it, multi-tenant Agent 365 management entered preview, giving administrators who govern multiple tenants a single view of agent inventory, activity, and risk across all of them. [4] Both capabilities exist because organizations routinely accumulate tenants and agents nobody centrally tracked, the same way Dahua's persistent backdoor account exists independently of whatever credential rotation an administrator believes has cleaned a device.

The throughline across both stories is that a reset, whether it is a factory reset on a compromised camera or a routine offboarding and tenant cleanup process, only removes what the process was designed to find. Anything stored outside that process's field of view survives it by default, not by attacker sophistication.


CAMERASWARM: THE BACKDOOR THAT SURVIVES A FACTORY RESET


Operation CameraSwarm ran three distinct compromise methods in parallel, each documented in enough detail in the attacker's own exposed working directory that Hunt.io could attribute exact device counts to each. Credential attacks, brute forcing weak or default logins, accounted for 12,324 of the compromised devices, the largest share and the least novel technique involved. [1]

The more consequential 1,923 devices were reached through CVE-2021-33044 and CVE-2021-33045, a pair of authentication bypass flaws that let an attacker construct malicious data packets to bypass device identity authentication entirely. [2] Both vulnerabilities are five years old. Dahua rates them 8.1 on its own severity scale; the US National Vulnerability Database rates them 9.8. The gap between those two scores is itself informative about how differently a vendor and an independent scoring body can weigh the same flaw, and organizations relying solely on vendor severity ratings for IoT patch prioritization should note that the more conservative external score was the more accurate one here. On these devices, the attacker planted the persistent backdoor account described above, one that Hunt.io confirmed survives a standard factory reset on most firmware versions. [2]

The remaining 283 devices were compromised through Dahua's own P2P cloud relay infrastructure, using nothing more than a device serial number, no IP address, no credentials, no network path required. This method reached cameras sitting behind network address translation that a conventional network based attack could never have touched directly. [2] Hunt.io's analysis names the tool involved p2pwn, which multiplexes three separate attack surfaces, a raw SDK channel used for the bypasses and backdoor deployment, an HTTP channel proxying to the device's embedded web server, and a JSON-RPC channel mimicking Dahua's internal management protocol, over a single relay tunnel. [1]

The operational lesson is specific to any organization running Dahua or similarly architected IoT camera deployments. Asset inventory needs to track device serial numbers, not just IP addresses and credentials, because serial number alone was sufficient for a meaningful share of this campaign's compromises. And any device suspected of carrying the persistent backdoor account should be treated as requiring a full firmware reflash or replacement, since a standard factory reset has been confirmed insufficient to remove it.


TENANTS AND AGENTS: GOVERNING WHAT YOU DIDN'T KNOW EXISTED


Entra Tenant Governance addresses a gap this series has not covered directly before: organizations that have grown through acquisition, regional expansion, or simple shadow IT frequently accumulate Entra tenants that no central team is actively managing. Reaching general availability, the capability brings an organization's tenants into a single view, applies centralized policy, and supports cross-tenant delegated administration specifically to close the blind spots those unmanaged tenants represent. [3] A shadow tenant is not a hypothetical risk category. It is an identity boundary with its own configuration, its own potential misconfigurations, and no one on the security team responsible for reviewing it, which makes it functionally similar to the unregistered local AI agents Vol. 13 covered through Defender's posture risk preview, an asset class that exists whether or not governance tooling has been pointed at it yet.

Multi-tenant Agent 365 management, now in preview alongside Tenant Governance, extends that same logic to AI agents specifically. Administrators who govern multiple tenants get a single console view of agent inventory, activity, and risk across every governed tenant, with the ability to identify risky agents and install or block agents directly from the Microsoft 365 admin center. [4] Access to agent activity and risky agent insights requires an Agent 365 license in the governed tenant, a licensing detail worth confirming before assuming coverage extends everywhere it appears to. [4]

Read together with Vol. 13's coverage of single tenant agent posture risk, this issue closes a scope gap rather than introducing a new capability. Vol. 13 covered discovering agents an organization did not know it had within one tenant. This issue covers discovering tenants an organization did not know it had at all, and the agents inside each of them. Any organization operating more than one Entra tenant, through acquisition, subsidiary structure, or historical sprawl, should treat enabling Tenant Governance as the first step, because the multi-tenant agent visibility depends on the tenant inventory being complete first.


THREE ITEMS REQUIRING IMMEDIATE ATTENTION


1. Inventory Dahua and similarly architected IoT camera deployments by serial number, and confirm firmware is current against CVE-2021-33044 and CVE-2021-33045. Any device suspected of compromise should be treated as requiring full firmware reflash or replacement, not a standard factory reset, given the confirmed persistence of the backdoor account. [1] [2]

2. Enable Microsoft Entra Tenant Governance to establish a complete tenant inventory before assuming any cross-tenant governance tool provides full coverage. A shadow tenant outside that inventory is invisible to every downstream governance capability, including multi-tenant agent management. [3]

3. Evaluate multi-tenant Agent 365 management if your organization governs more than one Entra tenant, and confirm Agent 365 licensing is in place in each governed tenant before relying on the agent activity and risk insights it provides. [4]


THE ROAD AHEAD


This issue's two stories describe the same structural failure at different scales. A factory reset assumes the reset process reaches everywhere an attacker could have stored something, and CameraSwarm shows that assumption breaking down at the level of a single device's firmware. A tenant and agent governance program assumes central visibility reaches every tenant and every agent an organization operates, and the gap Entra Tenant Governance and multi-tenant Agent 365 management were built to close shows that assumption breaking down at the level of an entire organization's identity footprint.

Neither gap is really about attacker sophistication. Dahua's persistent backdoor account is not an exotic technique, and shadow tenants are not the product of an unusually creative adversary. They persist because the visibility process that would have caught them was never built to look in that specific place. The organizations closing these gaps fastest are the ones auditing what their inventory and governance processes assume they cover, rather than what those processes actually reach.


FOR DISCUSSION

1. Operation CameraSwarm's persistent backdoor account survives a standard factory reset on most Dahua firmware. Does your IoT device remediation process account for persistence mechanisms that survive a reset, or does it treat a factory reset as equivalent to a clean device?

2. Entra Tenant Governance exists specifically to surface shadow tenants that accumulated outside central management. Does your organization have a current, complete inventory of every Entra tenant it operates, including ones created through acquisition or historical shadow IT?

3. Multi-tenant Agent 365 management requires an Agent 365 license in each governed tenant to surface agent activity and risk insights. If your organization governs multiple tenants, have you confirmed licensing is in place everywhere you assume agent visibility already exists?
