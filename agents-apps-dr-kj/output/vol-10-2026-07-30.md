THE LOOP CLOSES


Vol. 8 of this series covered MDASH, the AI system Microsoft uses to scan its own codebase and find vulnerabilities before attackers do, and the record patch volume that resulted. On July 27, 2026, Microsoft closed the second half of that loop. The company introduced Project Perception, an agentic security system, alongside MAI-Cyber-1-Flash, its first in-house cybersecurity model, which now also powers MDASH. [1] Where MDASH finds issues, Project Perception adds agents that assess which findings matter and agents that act on the ones that do. Detection was the first capability to go autonomous. Assessment and remediation are now following it.

The timing sits alongside a reminder of what autonomous defense is racing against. Since July 20, 2026, affiliates of the Cl0p ransomware operation have been running a data theft campaign against internet-exposed PTC Windchill and FlexPLM deployments in the aerospace, automotive, manufacturing, and retail sectors, using a vulnerability chain that was first weaponized as a zero-day in June. [2] The attackers did not need an AI system of their own. They needed six weeks between a patch becoming available and a security team applying it.

A third, smaller item closes out the month. Microsoft Defender Threat Intelligence reaches end of life as a standalone product on August 1, 2026. [3] Its capabilities are already available inside Defender and Sentinel at no additional cost, but organizations still paying for the standalone subscription have a narrow window to confirm the transition before the old SKU stops working.

Autonomous defense, an active ransomware campaign exploiting a patch gap, and a licensing deadline are three different scales of the same operating environment. All three reward organizations that act on information quickly and cost those that do not.


RED, BLUE, GREEN: THE AGENTIC SECURITY LOOP


Project Perception is built around three coordinated classes of AI agents operating in a continuous cycle. Red agents map attack paths and identify vulnerabilities across an organization's environment. Blue agents investigate what the red agents surface and determine which findings represent meaningful risk rather than noise. Green agents take corrective action, closing the gaps the first two stages identified. [1] The architecture is explicitly designed to keep human security teams in the loop for critical decisions while letting the machine speed portions of the cycle, mapping, triage, and routine remediation, run continuously rather than on a human review cadence.

The system runs on MAI-Cyber-1-Flash, a compact model tuned specifically for cybersecurity tasks rather than adapted from a general-purpose model. Microsoft reports the model scores 96 percent on CyberGym, an industry benchmark for offensive and defensive security tasks, 12 points above the previous best result, while running at roughly half the cost of the general-purpose model MDASH previously used. [1] The same model now powers both MDASH's vulnerability scanning and Project Perception's broader red, blue, green loop, which is why Vol. 8's coverage of record patch volume and this issue's coverage of autonomous remediation are describing the same underlying investment at two different points in its rollout.

Two operational details deserve attention before adoption. First, Project Perception enters public preview on August 3, 2026, delivered initially through Microsoft Defender, with additional Microsoft Security product integration planned afterward rather than available at launch. [4] Second, pricing is consumption based, metered in Security Compute Units rather than a flat per-seat or per-tenant license. [4] Scanning breadth and remediation activity become a budget line that scales with usage, which is a different planning exercise than the fixed licensing model security leaders are used to building cost forecasts around. Organizations evaluating the preview should scope a defined pilot with an expected SCU consumption range before expanding usage broadly.


CL0P MOVES AT MACHINE SPEED TOO


The Windchill and FlexPLM campaign is a useful counterpoint to how much of this issue's coverage is about AI accelerating defenders, because it shows attackers extracting comparable speed from a much simpler resource: the gap between patch availability and patch application.

The core vulnerability, CVE-2026-12569, is an unsafe deserialization flaw in PTC Windchill carrying a CVSS score of 9.3. PTC patched it on June 17, 2026, and confirmed exploitation in the wild the following day. [2] Cl0p affiliates chained it with a separate, lower-severity information disclosure flaw in the FlexPLM WSDL endpoint, CVSS 7.5, to achieve reliable unauthenticated remote code execution against internet-facing instances without requiring any credentials. [2] The vulnerability was added to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog in late June. [2]

What changed on July 20 was scale. Coordinated threat intelligence tracking from Ransom-ISAC, working with other research groups, documented the campaign shifting into active targeting of aerospace, automotive, manufacturing, and retail organizations running internet-exposed Windchill or FlexPLM instances, with attackers deploying JSP web shells to maintain access, then enumerating file systems and staging engineering and design data for exfiltration ahead of extortion demands. [2] Windchill and FlexPLM are product lifecycle management platforms that typically hold exactly the kind of proprietary design data that makes double extortion effective, and they are frequently deployed on Windows Server infrastructure inside the same networks these organizations also run Defender and Sentinel against.

Security teams do not need to run PTC's platforms directly to have a stake here. Any organization with PLM systems from any vendor exposed to the internet should treat this campaign as a prompt to verify patch status immediately, and Defender and Sentinel customers with any Windchill or FlexPLM footprint should hunt specifically for JSP web shell indicators and the known Cl0p-linked infrastructure that threat intelligence groups have already published. [2]


THREE ITEMS REQUIRING IMMEDIATE ATTENTION


1. Scope a Project Perception pilot ahead of its August 3 public preview. Because pricing is consumption based rather than flat licensed, security leaders should define an expected Security Compute Unit range and a bounded pilot scope, likely starting with the red and blue agent stages, before expanding into automated green agent remediation across production systems. [1] [4]

2. Verify patch status and hunt for compromise on any internet-exposed PLM platform. CVE-2026-12569 has been patched since June 17, but Cl0p's July 20 escalation shows the exploitation window remains active for anyone who has not applied it. Organizations with Windchill or FlexPLM exposure should confirm the patch, and Defender and Sentinel customers in affected industries should hunt for JSP web shell artifacts regardless of confirmed patch status. [2]

3. Confirm your organization's Microsoft Defender Threat Intelligence access ahead of the August 1 standalone retirement. Capabilities are already available through Defender and Sentinel at no additional cost, but standalone MDTI subscribers should confirm with their account team or partner that the transition is complete and should expect a credit memo for any remaining subscription term. [3]


THE ROAD AHEAD


The pattern across the last three issues of this series has been consistent: AI is compressing timelines for both attackers and defenders, and the organizations that keep pace are the ones that treat each new capability as an operational change to plan for, not a feature announcement to read about later. Vol. 8 covered AI-driven discovery producing more vulnerabilities than traditional triage capacity was built to absorb. Vol. 9 covered attackers persisting past the patch itself, through stolen cryptographic material. This issue covers Microsoft extending its own AI investment from finding problems to fixing them autonomously, arriving in the same month that a ransomware operation demonstrated how much damage a six week patch gap can still cause without any AI assistance at all.

Project Perception's consumption-based pricing is worth watching closely as adoption grows. A capability billed by usage rather than by seat changes how security leaders build a business case for expansion, and it rewards organizations that scope pilots deliberately over those that turn every available agent on at once. The Windchill and FlexPLM campaign is a reminder that the baseline threat, a known vulnerability with a known patch that has not yet been applied everywhere it needs to be, has not gone away simply because more sophisticated defensive tooling exists.


FOR DISCUSSION

1. Project Perception's red, blue, and green agents are designed to keep humans in the loop for critical decisions while automating the routine cycle of discovery, triage, and remediation. Where does your organization draw the line on what remediation actions an AI agent should be allowed to take without human sign off?

2. CVE-2026-12569 was patched on June 17, yet Cl0p's exploitation campaign escalated more than a month later, on July 20. What is the typical gap in your organization between a critical patch becoming available and it being applied across all internet-exposed instances of the affected platform?

3. Project Perception is priced by consumption in Security Compute Units rather than as a flat license. Has your security budget process been adapted to plan for a usage-metered AI security capability, or is it still built entirely around fixed per-seat licensing assumptions?
