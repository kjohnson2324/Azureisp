BUILT TO SURVIVE


On August 10, 2026, Microsoft Threat Intelligence published a detailed breakdown of DeadLock, a Rust-based ransomware operation first observed in July 2025 that has since accumulated more than 80 listed victims, concentrated in Europe, across IT, mining, transportation, manufacturing, hospitality, and consumer goods. [1] The detail that earned it a dedicated writeup is architectural rather than technical. DeadLock stores negotiation resources, victim communications, and portions of its data leak infrastructure inside Polygon blockchain smart contracts, paired with the decentralized Session messaging network, specifically to survive the kind of law enforcement server seizure that has disrupted other ransomware operations. [2] Infrastructure stored on a public blockchain cannot simply be taken offline.

The same week offered a study in the opposite failure mode. Vol. 8 of this series flagged a two-vulnerability SharePoint exploit chain built by Rapid7 Labs: CVE-2026-55040, a JWT authentication bypass patched in July, and a paired remote code execution flaw expected in August. Microsoft's August 11 Patch Tuesday delivered that second patch, CVE-2026-63520, closing the full chain. [4] But by August 14, researchers confirmed active exploitation of CVE-2026-55040 alone, the July patch, with a public proof of concept in circulation and attackers forging administrator sessions without a password on servers that had not applied a fix that had been available for a month. [3] Closing a chain does not help an organization that never closed the first link.

A third development points at a governance gap this series has tracked since Vol. 5. Microsoft Defender now assesses posture risk for AI agents in public preview, scoring both agents formally registered through Microsoft Agent 365 and unregistered local agents discovered directly on endpoint devices, based on configuration, access level, runtime behavior, and active alerts. [5] Attackers building infrastructure that survives takedown, a known vulnerability still exploited a month after a fix existed, and a new capability to see agents organizations did not know they had are three different views of the same problem: resilience and visibility on one side of the equation do not automatically produce them on the other.


DEADLOCK: RANSOMWARE THAT BUILT ITS OWN SAFE HOUSE


DeadLock's core encryption payload is unremarkable. It is Rust-based, uses standard double extortion tactics, encrypting victim environments while threatening public release of exfiltrated data, and has been deployed by multiple affiliated groups, including operators with observed overlap in the Lynx and INC ransomware ecosystems, consistent with a ransomware-as-a-service model where the malware is shared infrastructure and the operators running individual intrusions vary. [1]

What Microsoft's writeup emphasizes instead is the recovery and communication layer. DeadLock stores resources used throughout the extortion process inside Polygon smart contracts, effectively renting the durability of a public blockchain for infrastructure that would otherwise be a single point of failure a law enforcement operation could target. Session, a decentralized, metadata-resistant messaging network, handles ongoing communication with victims during negotiation. [1] [2] The combination means that even if a data leak site's conventional web hosting is seized, disrupting DeadLock's ability to pressure a victim into paying requires disrupting infrastructure that has no central operator to serve a takedown notice to.

This is not the first ransomware operation to experiment with resilient infrastructure, but it is a clear signal of direction. Security teams building incident response plans around an assumption that a coordinated takedown meaningfully ends an active extortion event should treat that assumption as increasingly unreliable for operations built this way. Negotiation posture, legal engagement, and communication planning during a DeadLock-style incident need to account for infrastructure that persists independent of any single server or hosting provider being taken down.


THE CHAIN CLOSES, THE EXPOSURE DOESN'T


The SharePoint exploit chain Rapid7 Labs built is worth revisiting for what it reveals about patch behavior, not just for the vulnerabilities themselves. CVE-2026-55040 exploits a flaw in SharePoint's JWT token validation pipeline: an attacker who knows a target's Active Directory Security ID or User Principal Name, both frequently discoverable through routine reconnaissance, can forge a token the server accepts as legitimate and operate with that user's privileges, including as a site administrator, without a password. [4] CVE-2026-63520, patched in August, is the paired remote code execution vulnerability that turns that forged administrative access into full code execution. [4]

Coverage of the chain's disclosure noted that Rapid7's research made use of AI tooling in the exploit development process, one more data point in the pattern Vol. 11 covered through David Weston's Black Hat keynote: offensive research that used to require weeks of manual work is compressing. [4] But the more immediate operational fact is simpler. Patching CVE-2026-55040 alone breaks the chain, since it is the first and necessary link, and that patch has existed since July. Active exploitation confirmed on August 14, more than a month later, means a meaningful population of SharePoint Server deployments had not applied a fix that fully neutralized the threat on its own. [3] The August patch closing the RCE half is good news for organizations that had already handled the July half. It does nothing for the ones that had not.


GOVERNING THE AGENTS YOU ALREADY HAVE


Defender's new AI agent posture risk capability extends a thread this series picked up in Vol. 5 with Purview Insider Risk Management for agents, and again in Vol. 10 with Sentinel's Agent Identities connector. Each of those addressed a different piece of the same underlying problem: organizations are accumulating AI agents, some formally deployed and governed, some created ad hoc by individual employees or embedded in tools nobody centrally tracked, faster than existing security processes were built to account for.

The posture risk preview scores agents on configuration, the access and permissions they hold, observed runtime activity, the endpoint and user context they operate within, and any active alerts associated with them, producing a risk level intended to help security teams prioritize which agents need attention first. [5] Critically, the scope includes agents registered through Microsoft Agent 365 and agents discovered locally on endpoints that were never formally registered anywhere, which is the population most organizations have the least visibility into today.

The practical value of this capability depends entirely on organizations actually running the discovery step. A posture score is not useful for an agent nobody knew existed until the discovery scan finds it. Security teams should treat enabling this preview as a discovery exercise first and a prioritization exercise second, because the first honest inventory of an organization's actual AI agent footprint is often the most useful output on its own.


THREE ITEMS REQUIRING IMMEDIATE ATTENTION


1. Confirm both CVE-2026-55040 and CVE-2026-63520 are patched on every on-premises SharePoint instance. Treat the July patch as unfinished business if it has not been verified, given confirmed active exploitation and a public proof of concept as of August 14. [3]

2. Update ransomware incident response planning to account for decentralized extortion infrastructure. If DeadLock or a similar operation using blockchain-backed or decentralized communication infrastructure is involved in an incident, plan for negotiation and leak pressure to continue independent of any takedown action against conventional hosting. [1] [2]

3. Enable Defender's AI agent posture risk preview as a discovery exercise. Run the assessment across both Agent 365 registered agents and local endpoint discovered agents before focusing on risk prioritization, since the value of the capability depends on first establishing an accurate inventory of agents the organization did not know it had. [5]


THE ROAD AHEAD


This issue's three items share a lesson this series keeps returning to from different directions. DeadLock shows attackers investing deliberately in infrastructure resilience. Microsoft is investing deliberately in agent visibility. Neither investment closes the gap that actually keeps costing organizations: the operational follow through between a fix or a capability becoming available and an organization actually using it. CVE-2026-55040 was patchable in July. The gap that mattered in August was not technical.

The pattern holds whether the capability in question is a security patch, a new detection capability, or a governance tool for AI agents. Availability is necessary and not sufficient. The organizations that treat each new disclosure this series covers as an immediate operational task, not a report to file, are the ones closing gaps before the next issue documents who did not.


FOR DISCUSSION

1. DeadLock's use of blockchain-backed infrastructure is designed specifically to survive law enforcement takedown efforts. Does your organization's ransomware incident response plan account for extortion pressure continuing after a takedown action, or does it assume disruption of attacker infrastructure ends the immediate threat?

2. CVE-2026-55040 was patched in July, yet active exploitation was confirmed in mid-August with administrator sessions forged without a password. What is your process for verifying that a critical patch, once released, has actually been applied across every affected instance, rather than assuming deployment matches disclosure?

3. Microsoft Defender's new posture risk preview covers both formally registered AI agents and agents discovered locally on endpoints that were never registered anywhere. Does your organization know how many AI agents are currently operating in your environment outside of any formal registration process?
