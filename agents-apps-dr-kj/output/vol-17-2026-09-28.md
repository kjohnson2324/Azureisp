THE CEREMONY IS THE TARGET


On September 23, 2026, Microsoft announced the Integrated Security Operations Center, or ISOC, in Microsoft Defender, unifying SIEM and threat protection into a single system built specifically for what Microsoft calls the agentic era. [1] It is the clearest structural evidence yet that the Sentinel to Defender portal convergence this series first flagged in Vol. 3 was never just a licensing consolidation. It was the foundation for exactly this: one shared workspace where the agentic architecture introduced with Project Perception in July can operate directly against the same data humans use to investigate and respond.

The same week, Microsoft disclosed a threat intelligence campaign that undercuts any temptation to treat modernized defense as a solved problem. Since May 2026, Microsoft has tracked attackers impersonating IT staff and telling employees they need to update a passkey, an MFA method, or a single sign-on setting, directing them to counterfeit Microsoft sign-in pages that harvest the resulting authorization. [3] [4] The campaign has now been attributed across at least nine distinct threat clusters, including Storm-3121, Storm-3032, UNC6671, Cordial Spider, and ShinyHunters, each running the identical lure independently. [3] Passkeys were built specifically to resist phishing at the cryptographic level, and they are doing exactly that. What attackers found instead was the ceremony around them, the moment a user is asked to update or re-register a credential, which no cryptography protects on its own.

A third item carries over from Vol. 16 without resolution. ShieldCrash, the Microsoft Defender proof of concept that bypasses the ShieldBreak patch, remains unacknowledged and unpatched three weeks after its September 9 disclosure. [5] Structural progress on one front and an unresolved active risk on another are, at this point in the series, simply the normal operating condition.


ISOC: ONE SYSTEM FOR PEOPLE AND AGENTS


ISOC's core change is architectural rather than cosmetic. Security information and event management and extended detection and response have functioned as adjacent but distinct systems for most of this series' run, with Vol. 6 and Vol. 8 both noting Sentinel capabilities migrating toward the Defender portal in stages. ISOC completes that convergence by giving people and Microsoft's security agents a shared foundation to see, understand, and act across an environment without switching between separate systems to do it. [2] The framing matters: Microsoft is explicit that the point is not consolidation for its own sake, but giving the red, blue, and green agent loop introduced with Project Perception the same operational surface a human analyst works from, so agent actions and human judgment apply to identical data rather than parallel copies of it.

Three concrete changes accompany the announcement. Data retention for eligible customers extends from 30 to 90 days across Defender data, Azure Activity, and Office 365 Activity, effective November 15, 2026. [2] Connector coverage expands to more than 500 sources starting October 1, priced through a pay as you go ingestion model at 2.40 dollars per gigabyte. [2] And the capability is positioned as a benefit included with Microsoft 365 E5 and E7 licensing rather than a new standalone product requiring separate procurement. [2]

The pay as you go connector pricing deserves specific budget attention. Unlike a flat licensing tier, ingestion cost scales directly with how much data an organization pulls in from its expanded connector footprint, which mirrors the consumption based pricing this series flagged for Project Perception in Vol. 10. Security leaders planning to take advantage of the October 1 connector expansion should model expected ingestion volume against the per gigabyte rate before enabling broad connector coverage, the same discipline Vol. 10 recommended for scoping an AI agent pilot before letting it run unconstrained.


THE CEREMONY IS THE TARGET


The passkey campaign's technical pattern is now familiar enough across this series to name directly. An attacker impersonates IT support or a helpdesk function and tells an employee a security credential needs to be updated, in this case a passkey, MFA method, or SSO configuration. The employee is directed to a bogus domain hosting a counterfeit Microsoft sign in page. Once the update completes on the attacker's infrastructure rather than Microsoft's, the attacker adds their own authentication method to the compromised account for persistence, then conducts high volume activity through Microsoft Graph, downloads from SharePoint and OneDrive, and mailbox collection through REST APIs. [3]

This is the third time this series has documented attackers succeeding against a strong authentication mechanism by targeting its enrollment or update ceremony instead of its cryptography. Vol. 9 covered Kali365 abusing the OAuth device code flow. Vol. 11 covered CaptiveCrunch using the identical device code technique on behalf of a Russian state intelligence sub-cluster. This issue covers nine separate financially motivated groups independently running the same structural attack against passkey and MFA enrollment. The specific mechanism changes. The target does not. Whenever an authentication system requires a legitimate moment for a user to register, re-register, or update a credential, that moment becomes the attack surface, regardless of how resistant the underlying cryptography is to conventional phishing.

The defensive implication is specific and actionable. Organizations should treat any unsolicited request to update a passkey, MFA method, or SSO setting as a named, trained scenario, distinct from generic phishing awareness, the same recommendation Vol. 12 made for Operation Dream Job's recruiting lure. Self-service authentication method changes should require verified out of band confirmation rather than trusting an in-session prompt alone, and any newly added authentication method on an account should generate an alert reviewed against a legitimate change request, not assumed to be routine.


THREE ITEMS REQUIRING IMMEDIATE ATTENTION


1. Train employees and helpdesk staff specifically on the passkey and MFA update impersonation scenario, distinct from generic phishing training. Require verified out of band confirmation before any self-service authentication method change is accepted as legitimate. [3] [4]

2. Audit for unexpected authentication methods added to user accounts, the primary persistence mechanism in this campaign, and review Microsoft Graph, SharePoint, OneDrive, and mailbox access logs for anomalous volume following any recent authentication method change. [3]

3. Model expected data ingestion volume against ISOC's 2.40 dollar per gigabyte connector pricing before enabling the expanded 500 plus connector catalog on October 1, and plan governance review ahead of the November 15 retention increase rather than assuming expanded retention applies automatically without cost and access implications. [2]


THE ROAD AHEAD


ISOC is a genuine architectural maturation, closing a gap this series has tracked since its earliest issues between a fragmented SIEM and XDR experience and the unified, agent ready operating surface Project Perception's vision required. It is reasonable to treat this as real progress in giving defenders, human and automated, a shared foundation to work from.

The passkey campaign is the counterweight. Nine independent threat clusters converging on the same technique within months of each other confirms that ceremony targeting, not cryptography breaking, is now a commodity tradecraft category the way device code phishing became one after Vol. 9. Defensive architecture and detection capability keep maturing. So does the specific, narrow class of human interaction attackers have identified as the reliable way around each new mechanism designed to stop them. Closing that gap requires treating enrollment and credential update moments as a permanent, named risk category, not a one-time awareness campaign responding to this quarter's specific lure.


FOR DISCUSSION

1. This is the third technique this series has covered where attackers succeed against strong authentication by targeting its enrollment or update ceremony rather than its cryptography. Does your security awareness program treat credential update requests as a distinct, named risk category, or does it fold them into generic phishing training?

2. Nine separate threat clusters are independently running the identical passkey impersonation technique. Does your organization require verified out of band confirmation before accepting any self-service change to a user's authentication methods?

3. ISOC's expanded connector catalog is priced by ingestion volume rather than a flat license. Has your organization modeled the cost of the connector footprint it plans to enable on October 1, or will that decision be made after ingestion costs are already being incurred?
